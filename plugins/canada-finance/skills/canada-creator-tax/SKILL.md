# Tax for creators in Canada

## Income (CRA treats a regular creator activity as a business)
Sponsorships, ad revenue (YouTube, TikTok), affiliate commissions, memberships and tips, merch, digital products, and gifted products, trips and perks received for promotion, at fair market value. Report on form T2125 with your T1 return. Every dollar counts; there's no minimum.

## GST/HST
- Online content and services are generally taxable supplies.
- Once taxable supplies exceed $30,000 over four consecutive calendar quarters (or in a single quarter), you're no longer a small supplier: register, then charge and remit GST/HST. The rate depends on the customer's province (Ontario HST 13%).
- Below $30,000 you can register voluntarily to claim input tax credits (GST/HST paid on cameras, software, etc.).
- When registered, tell platforms (e.g. YouTube) so they handle GST/HST correctly.
- Payments from foreign brands for services can be zero-rated exports; check the conditions.

## Expenses usually deductible (keep receipts)
Equipment (capital cost allowance, not all at once), editing software and subscriptions, home office share, phone and internet share, props and wardrobe used only for content, travel for shoots, contractors (editors, managers), ads for your own channel. Personal-use items aren't deductible.

## Other
- CPP: self-employed pay both the employee and employer share on net self-employment income.
- No tax is withheld on creator income: set aside roughly 25 to 30% of each payment.
- Instalments are required if net tax owing is over $3,000 in the current year and either of the two previous years.
- US brands may send a W-8BEN request; as a Canadian, the treaty usually means no US withholding on services performed in Canada.

## Records
Track every deal: date, brand, deliverable, amount, GST/HST charged, value of gifted items, payment date. Keep contracts six years.

End with: confirm with a Canadian accountant, especially for GST/HST registration and incorporation questions.
